01 · The brief

One question:
what should a night cost?

Hosts price by feel, guests can't tell a fair rate from an inflated one, and regulators can't see what short stays earn. The brief was narrow: predict the nightly price of 3,000 Melbourne listings from 7,000 that had prices, scored by mean absolute error on a hidden test set.

I treated the model as an engine for a wider question. Once you know what a listing should cost, the gap between that and what it asks says something different to each person in the market.

Who a fair price serves

Hosts & homeownersA rate grounded in comparable listings, and a dollar value on upgrades before spending on them.
GuestsA way to tell a good deal from an inflated price.
PlatformsFlags for broken or extreme prices, and a starting price for new listings with no history.
Policy makersAn estimate of what short stays earn, where, and how many homes they occupy.

What made the data hard

  • SkewMedian price $172 a night, 99th percentile $1,100, maximum $145,160. One bad price can outweigh thousands of good ones.
  • ShiftHost response time was missing for 0% of training listings but 24.6% of test listings; review scores for 0.3% versus 10%. Dropping gaps would have dropped a quarter of the predictions.
  • Mess2,362 distinct amenity strings, free-text descriptions, and bathrooms written as "1.5 shared baths".

02 · Data engineering

Sharpen the axe:
a five-stage pipeline.

Most of the work went into the data, not the algorithm. I wrote preprocessing as five stages, each owning its own columns, and every statistic is learned on the training listings only and then applied unchanged to the test listings.

01

Clean

Make every field machine-readable.

  • Price and rate strings to numbers ("$1,250.00", "98%")
  • Bathroom text to a count plus a shared or private flag
  • Each coordinate reverse-geocoded to suburb and council (Geopy), cached as a lookup table
02

Impute

Logic first, statistics second.

  • Room and property type inferred from description text
  • Skew-aware mean or median, and modes, learned on train only
  • Property type → its most common room type as a fallback
03

Encode

Turn categories into signal.

  • Smoothed target encoding for property type and council, on price tiers
  • Ordinal host response time; top-10 amenity flags
  • One-hot bath type and host location, aligned to train columns
04

Engineer

Add what raw columns can't say.

  • Haversine distance to the CBD and four distance zones
  • Transit stops within 500 m (OpenStreetMap and a BallTree)
  • VADER sentiment, 31 luxury keywords, amenity-richness ratios
05

Cap

Tame outliers without leaking.

  • 1st and 99th percentile caps learned on train
  • Applied identically to train and test
  • Output: 175 model-ready features
Orchestration · Task 2 notebook 
def preprocess_pipeline(train_df, test_df):
    train_df = step_1_cleaning(train_df)   # formats, amenities, suburb & council
    test_df  = step_1_cleaning(test_df)
    train_df, test_df = stage_2_imputation(train_df, test_df)
    # ↑ statistics learned on train, applied to both
    train_df, test_df = stage_3_encoding(train_df, test_df)
    train_df, test_df = stage_4_engineering(train_df, test_df)
    train_df, test_df = stage_5_transformation(train_df, test_df)
    return train_df, test_df   # saved as 7,000 × 177 and 3,000 × 176
Leakage rules the pipeline enforces
  • Imputation values come from training listings only
  • Target encoders are fitted on training price tiers only
  • Amenity vocabulary and council density are learned on train, mapped to test
  • Outlier caps come from training quantiles
  • One-hot test columns are re-aligned to the training schema

03 · Modelling

Tune the features,
not just the trees.

  1. Model the log of price

    Training on log(1 + price) tames the skew, so a $20 miss on a $100 room and a $200 miss on a $1,000 house count for about the same.

  2. Rank every feature first

    A LightGBM model ranks all 175 features by mean absolute SHAP value; XGBoost and Random Forest rank them by their own importance scores.

  3. Let Optuna choose how many to keep

    The number of top features is a hyperparameter, searched together with depth, learning rate and regularisation by a TPE sampler: 50 trials on 5-fold cross-validation for the boosters, 30 trials on 3 folds for Random Forest. LightGBM kept 108 features, XGBoost 111, Random Forest 107.

  4. Match the loss to the metric

    XGBoost was trained on absolute error (reg:absoluteerror), the same yardstick the leaderboard used.

04 · Post-competition check

Why $51 in validation
became $109 on the leaderboard.

Cross-validation said the models missed by about $51 a night; the private leaderboard said $109. My first read, in the notebooks, was that tabular data can't see what guests see, such as photos. Re-checking after the competition showed most of the gap came from how the error was measured.

  1. 01

    Capped answers

    Stage 5 capped every training price at $1,100, the 99th percentile. Cross-validation therefore scored predictions against capped prices, while the leaderboard scored them against prices as listed.

  2. 02

    Placeholder prices

    Ten listings ask more than $5,000 a night: a two-guest apartment at $145,160, a private room at $104,983, and one titled "Test listing Do Not Book". They are 0.14% of listings but 51% of all absolute error.

  3. 03

    Small halves, big swings

    A single placeholder like the $145,160 one would add about $97 to the MAE of a 1,500-listing leaderboard half on its own. That is the likeliest reason public (~$268) and private (~$109) scores disagreed so sharply while the ranking held.

The lesson: check the yardstick before blaming the model. The luxury gap is real and photos could help close it, but the headline gap was a data-quality problem. That became the platform's first action below: catch placeholder prices when they're entered.

Re-check method: scikit-learn HistGradientBoostingRegressor (absolute-error loss, log target) on the same 175 engineered features, 5-fold cross-validation with seed 42. It reproduces the notebooks' CV MAE ($51.68 against $51.07–51.35); only the prices it is scored against change.

05 · What the data says

Six findings on
price, demand and trust.

Fair-price model

The same engineered features minus calendar and review counts, so it estimates what similar listings charge without peeking at demand. Each listing is priced out of fold.

Hedonic regression

Log price on size, type, location, 28 amenities and host signals: 6,990 listings, R² = 0.64, robust standard errors. It puts a percentage on each attribute for otherwise comparable listings.

Read with care

A March 2023 snapshot. Effects are associations, not proof that adding a feature raises the price. Booked nights and revenue are estimates and are labelled as such.
01

Location is a U, not a slope.

Against a comparable listing in the middle ring (7–15 km out), a central listing asks 17% more and an inner-ring one 11% more; the outer suburbs ask 9% less. Then prices climb again: the Yarra Ranges asks 28% more, the most of any area, and is the dearest council by median price ($270 a night).

Olinda ($319), Brighton ($287) and Healesville ($271) top the suburbs with 40 or more listings. The city premium also grows with size: the median one-bedroom home in the central ring asks 14% more than in the middle ring, and the median three-bedroom 57% more.

So whatMelbourne has two premium markets, not one: the city and the hills. Pricing, policy and tourism plans that treat distance from the CBD as a simple discount will misjudge both.
02

Size sets the base; bathrooms refine it.

Property type, guest capacity, bedrooms and bathrooms are the model's four most influential features. The regression puts a number on each: an extra bedroom adds 18% and an extra bathroom 14% to an otherwise comparable listing, while a shared bathroom costs 27%.

So whatFor homeowners deciding what to list, a second bathroom is worth almost as much as another bedroom, and sharing one is the single biggest discount after renting a room instead of the whole home.

03

Some amenities pay; many don't move the price.

A fireplace (+18%), allowing pets (+14%) and a clothes dryer (+11%) lead the premiums. At the median price of $172 a night that is about $31, $23 and $18. Air conditioning adds 8% and is missing from 15% of listings; only 16% of listings allow pets.

Pools, balconies, workspaces and views show no clear premium once size and location are held equal. Negative signs on near-universal items say more about which listings lack them: listings without a washer or heating are more often serviced apartments and hotel rooms (11–12% against 4–5%), which charge more for their size.

So whatBefore spending on an upgrade, check whether comparable listings that have it actually charge more. Many visible extras don't.
04

Trust fills the calendar; it doesn't raise the price.

Superhosts, 43% of listings, charge 5.5% less than comparable listings, yet log 22 reviews a year against 7 and rate 4.89 against 4.70. Superhost status requires steady bookings, so part of the activity gap is built in; the surprise is the price.

A rating of 4.9 or above does carry a 13% premium, and instant-book listings charge 15% more than comparable request-to-book ones.

So whatService earns occupancy and reviews first and price later. For guests, superhost listings are the best value in the market.

05

Overpricing shows up as empty nights.

23% of listings ask 20% or more above their fair price. They average 12.9 open nights in the next 30 days against 10.0 for fairly priced listings, and an estimated 26% fewer booked nights a year (89 against 121). Their median yearly revenue is no higher ($17.6K against $17.1K): the extra rate is spent on empty nights.

At the other end, 235 listings are booked solid yet sit a median 29% ($44 a night) below their fair price (three or fewer open nights in the next month and 12+ reviews in the past year): up to about $7,800 a year each if demand holds at the higher rate. New listings lean the wrong way: those with fewer than three reviews ask 22% more than comparable listings, and a third sit 20% or more above fair price.

So whatThe fair price is a usable signal: a listing far above it should expect gaps in the calendar, and a full calendar below it is money left on the table.
06

A professional market with a regional engine.

61% of listings belong to hosts with more than one listing, and 21% to operators with ten or more. The biggest operators (21+ listings) run 91% entire homes and rate lower: 4.67 against 4.88 for single-listing hosts.

An estimated 1,796 entire homes, a third of entire-home listings, were booked 180 or more nights in the year, led by the City of Melbourne (738), the Yarra Ranges (215) and Port Phillip (203). Across all 10,000 listings, with the 3,000 hidden ones priced by the model, bookings come to an estimated $206M a year over about one million nights. Among the 7,000 priced listings, the Yarra Ranges holds 9% of listings but earns 15% of booking revenue.

So whatShort stays are partly a housing question in the inner city and mostly a tourism asset in the hills. One policy setting won't fit both.

06 · Short-term actions, next 0–6 months

What each stakeholder
can do now.

Most of a listing's price is set by what it is and where it is. What hosts control is where they sit against their fair price and which upgrades they buy.

01

Price inside the fair band

Do

Check your nightly rate against comparable listings (same size, type and council) every month, and stay within 20% of it.

Why

Listings 20%+ above fair price average 12.9 open nights in the next 30 days against 10.0, and about 26% fewer booked nights a year.

TrackOpen nights in the next 30 days
02

Raise the rate when you're booked solid

Do

If the next month is full, lift the price 5–10% and check the calendar again after two weeks.

Why

235 listings are booked solid yet a median $44 a night (29%) under fair price: up to about $7,800 a year each.

TrackRevenue per available night
03

Upgrade what guests pay for

Do

Prioritise air conditioning, a dryer and a dishwasher, and consider allowing pets. Don't count on a pool, balcony or workspace to lift the rate.

Why

Comparable listings earn +8% with air conditioning (15% lack it), +11% with a dryer, +4% with a dishwasher and +14% when pets are allowed (only 16% allow them).

TrackPayback = cost ÷ (premium × booked nights)
04

Launch at fair price, then climb

Do

Price a new listing at or just below fair value until the first reviews arrive, then step up.

Why

Listings with fewer than three reviews ask 22% more than comparable ones and average 12.8 open nights against 9.8. A rating of 4.9+ later earns +13%.

TrackDays to the first three reviews

The same fair-price gap that warns hosts helps guests: about one listing in five is priced 20% or more above what comparable listings charge.

01

Benchmark before you book

Do

Compare a shortlisted listing with three or four similar ones nearby, and question premiums above 20% that have no obvious reason.

Why

23% of listings ask 20%+ above fair price; 18% sit 20%+ below it.

TrackPrice against comparable listings
02

Start with superhosts

Do

Filter for superhosts first, then compare on price.

Why

They charge 5.5% less than comparable listings and rate 4.89 against 4.70.

TrackRating per dollar
03

Stay one ring out

Do

Look 7–15 km from the CBD, near a train or tram line.

Why

A comparable listing there costs about 15% less than in the central ring and 10% less than in the inner ring.

TrackTotal trip cost, fares included
04

Book on request when you can

Do

Don't filter to instant book by default; message hosts when dates allow.

Why

Instant-book listings charge about 15% more than comparable request-to-book ones.

TrackSaving per night

The platform sees every price before guests do. A fair-price model at listing time fixes bad data, guides new hosts and makes value visible.

01

Catch placeholder prices at entry

Do

Flag any nightly price above five times its fair estimate before it goes live, and ask the host to confirm it.

Why

10 listings above $5,000, such as $145,160 for a two-guest apartment, are 0.14% of listings but 51% of model error, and they distort search, market statistics and any revenue estimate.

TrackFlags resolved within 7 days
02

Show hosts a fair-price band

Do

Show the band in the host dashboard, and make it the default suggestion for new listings.

Why

A third of listings with fewer than three reviews sit 20%+ above fair price, just when they need bookings most.

TrackShare of listings within 20% of fair price
03

Reward value, not just price

Do

Badge well-rated listings priced at or below fair value in search results.

Why

Superhost listings are already cheaper and better rated; a value badge makes that visible to guests who don't compare listings themselves.

TrackConversion of badged listings
04

Separate booked from blocked nights

Do

Record whether each unavailable night is booked or blocked by the host, and share it in aggregate.

Why

Today's calendars can't tell them apart, so occupancy, revenue and any levy base have to be estimated from reviews.

TrackShare of nights with a known status

Victoria's 7.5% short stay levy has applied to stays under 28 days since 1 January 2025, and owners corporations can now ban short stays by a 75% vote. This snapshot predates both, so it doubles as a pre-levy baseline.

01

Count homes, not listings

Do

Track entire homes booked 180+ nights a year, by council, every quarter.

Why

1,796 entire homes in the sample, a third of entire-home listings, cross that line; the City of Melbourne (738), Yarra Ranges (215) and Port Phillip (203) lead.

TrackEntire homes booked 180+ nights
02

Benchmark the levy against a model

Do

Compare collections with a model-based revenue estimate for each council, so gaps point to where compliance or data are weak.

Why

On the sample's estimated $155M of short-stay bookings, 7.5% is about $11.6M a year (about $15M across all 10,000 listings). The State Revenue Office reported $19.1M statewide in the levy's first six months.

TrackCollected ÷ modelled, by council
03

Treat hills and high-rises differently

Do

Separate tourism-led markets from housing-pressure markets when setting rules and spending levy revenue.

Why

The Yarra Ranges earns 15% of revenue from 9% of listings, and 9% of its listings belong to operators with 10+ listings. In the City of Melbourne that share is 39%.

TrackEntire homes held by 10+ listing operators
04

Use the 28-night line for renters

Do

Encourage mid-term stays of 28+ nights for students, key workers and people between homes.

Why

Only 2.3% of listings require 28+ nights. Such stays fall outside the levy and price about 10% below comparable short stays.

TrackListings with 28+ night minimums

The levy's future is an issue at the November 2026 state election; actions 01, 03 and 04 hold whatever happens to it. Levy facts: State Revenue Office Victoria, short stay levy and its 2024–25 annual review.

What carries over

Check the yardstick before blaming the model

The $51-to-$109 gap was mostly capped answers and placeholder prices, not missing photos.

Engineering beats algorithms

On the same features, XGBoost and LightGBM finished $0.88 apart. The pipeline did the heavy lifting.

Prediction and explanation are different jobs

XGBoost won the leaderboard; a regression tells a host what a dryer is worth. A good analysis uses both.

Label every estimate

Occupancy, revenue and levy figures here come from models, and each one says so.

What I'd change next time

  • Fit target encoders inside each cross-validation fold, and score validation on raw prices with a placeholder rule.
  • Add listing photos, calendar dates and events; try CatBoost and a stacked ensemble.
  • Refresh with post-levy data from 2025–26 to measure what the levy changed.
  • Test causal claims properly: a snapshot can't show that adding a dryer raises the price.
